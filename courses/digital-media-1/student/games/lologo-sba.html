1.1Introduction and Background

Overview: brief introduction · 1 mark (Proposal). ✓ Met because, in two short paragraphs, it explains what a lolo is, why the problem matters to residents, visitors and owners, and why an app suits this audience (digital natives as customers, digital immigrants as owners), linked to the group’s own data.

In Sint Maarten a lolo is a roadside food stand: a drum grill, a counter and a hand-painted sign selling johnny cakes, saltfish, BBQ chicken, ribs, conch and fresh juices. For residents it is the quickest affordable hot meal, and for visitors one of the island’s most authentic food experiences. Most lolos are family businesses with no website or card machine.

At peak times the system breaks down. Queues grow, dishes sell out unseen, hours are rarely posted online and cash-only payment turns customers away. Yet almost every customer carries a smartphone. Palfrey and Gasser (2008) describe “digital natives” who expect services on demand, while many owners are digital immigrants. A solution must be fast for customers and simple for owners.

1.1.1Problem Statement

Rationale: overview of the problem being solved · 1 mark (Research/Pitch). ✓ Met because the statement names who is affected, what happens and what it costs, without describing the solution. Every number traces to Figure 27, Table 25 or Table 27.

Lolo customers in Sint Maarten, both residents and visitors, cannot check opening times or dish availability, order ahead or pay without cash. As a result they face long queues and wasted trips, and lolo owners lose sales at their busiest times.

The evidence comes from the group’s October 2025 research (Appendix 4.3). Of 84 respondents, 61.9% named long queues, 48.8% cannot tell whether a lolo is open, and 44.0% are stopped by cash-only payment. Of the 22 visitors, 68.2% had given up finding a lolo. The group timed a 25-minute average wait on a Friday night, and 15 customers walked away across two sessions.

1.2Executive Summary

Overview: summary of the content · 2 marks (Proposal). ✓ Met because, in 166 words, it summarises the problem, the solution, the audience, the method, the cost, the test results and the ask, and every figure matches the research, budget and testing sections. Written last.

Sint Maarten’s roadside lolos serve much-loved local food, but customers queue for 20 minutes or more, cannot see whether a lolo is open or a dish has sold out, and usually must pay cash. In a survey of 84 residents and visitors, 61.9% named long queues and 68.2% of visitors had given up trying to find a lolo.

LoloGo® is a mobile app for students, young workers, visitors and lolo owners. Customers find nearby lolos, see live open and sold-out status, build a plate, pay by card, wallet or cash, and track their order. Owners manage orders with three large buttons. The working HTML prototype, built in four two-week agile sprints for US$150.70, reached 80% task success and a usability score of 73.5, and 100% on the redesigned tasks in version 1.1. Launch costs US$18,000, and the group asks an angel investor for US$7,000 and five lolos for a March–May 2026 pilot. Expected outcomes are shorter waits, fewer wasted trips and more sales for small businesses.

J. Brooks: I write it on the board, but they only see it when they reach the front. Then they’re vexed with me, after waiting twenty minutes.

4.2.4D. Usability test script and SUS questionnaire

Script (read aloud): “Thank you for helping. We are testing the app, not you, so there are no wrong answers. Please think aloud as you go. We will time each task but will not help unless you are stuck for two minutes. You can stop at any time.” Participants then completed tasks T1–T6 (Table 20) on an Android phone, with the screen recorded (no face).

SUS questionnaire (Brooke, 1996), adapted by replacing “system” with “app”. Each item is answered from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree):

  1. I think that I would like to use this app frequently.
  2. I found the app unnecessarily complex.
  3. I thought the app was easy to use.
  4. I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this app.
  5. I found the various functions in this app were well integrated.
  6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in this app.
  7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use this app very quickly.
  8. I found the app very cumbersome to use.
  9. I felt very confident using the app.
  10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this app.

Scoring: for odd items subtract 1 from the answer; for even items subtract the answer from 5. Add the ten results and multiply by 2.5 to give a score from 0 to 100.

4.5.6F. Individual reflections

Candidate A (project manager and prototype lead). Running the sprints taught me that a plan is only useful if it changes. In Sprint 2 we did not finish the promo code, and I wanted to push the deadline. Instead we moved it to Sprint 3 and kept the review date, which kept the whole schedule on track. Building the HTML prototype was the hardest part. The tracking map took three attempts before the scooter moved smoothly along the route. The usability test humbled me. I was sure the area picker was obvious, but two of five people never found it. Watching P3 search the screen for the town name made me understand why designers test with real users. If I did this again, I would run a five-minute hallway test after every sprint, not only at the end. I am most proud that version 1.1 was measurably better: 100% success on both fixed tasks.

Candidate B (research and testing lead). I had never designed a questionnaire before, and my first draft had leading questions such as “Don’t you hate waiting?”. The feedback helped me rewrite them neutrally. Getting visitors to answer was the biggest challenge. Our first 40 responses were nearly all students, so we added QR posters at the lolos, which brought in 23 more varied responses. I learned that how you collect data shapes the answers you get. The interview gave us the most memorable evidence, the owner’s “a third of my price”, and it changed our business model from commission to a flat subscription. In testing, I learned to stay quiet and let people struggle. The pauses taught us more than any question. Next time I would include more older users and test outdoors in sunlight, which is where the owner board will really be used.

Candidate C (brand and media lead). I started with a logo of a map pin and a fork, but it looked like every food app. Moving to the sun ring and drum grill made the brand feel like Sint Maarten. Checking contrast showed that white text on our flame red only passes at large sizes, so I kept flame for big buttons and prices. Filming at the lolo at 7 a.m. was tiring but worth it. Real smoke and a real queue are more convincing than any stock video. My biggest problem was that our first cut of the inserts was 52 seconds, which left too little time for the business slides. Cutting it to 40 seconds hurt, but the pitch became tighter and fitted inside five minutes. I learned to plan audio as carefully as video. A simple room-tone recording saved our voice-over. In future I would storyboard the pitch before writing the script, not after.
