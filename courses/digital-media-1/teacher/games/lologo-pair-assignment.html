Introduction

A strong introduction starts from a local, observable problem and says why it matters now. Compare the school’s sample, which opened with a general global issue. This one also says what the group does not yet know, and that leads naturally into Collection of Data.

In Sint Maarten a lolo is a roadside food stand: a drum grill, a counter and a hand-painted sign. Lolos sell johnny cakes and saltfish in the morning, BBQ chicken and ribs at lunch, and conch, plantain and fresh juices into the evening. They are part of the island’s culture and an important source of income for small family businesses.

Anyone who buys from a lolo at a busy time knows the problems. Queues stretch towards the road. Popular dishes sell out while people are still waiting. Opening hours change from day to day and are rarely posted online, so customers drive to a lolo that turns out to be closed. Most lolos accept cash only, so customers without cash walk away. Visitors hear about lolos from taxi drivers and hotel staff but cannot find them on a map.

At the same time, nearly every customer carries a smartphone and already orders other things through apps and WhatsApp. Palfrey and Gasser (2008) describe a generation of “digital natives” who expect information and services to be available on demand. Many lolo owners, however, use their phones mainly for calls and WhatsApp. This proposal suggests a digital solution, LoloGo, and explains how the group will collect data to find out whether customers and owners really need it.

Description

Good descriptions say what the product does, step by step, and keep to what a student team can actually build. Note the honest scope line: a working prototype, not a launched business.

LoloGo is a planned mobile app that lets customers order food from local lolos for pick-up or delivery. The working idea has five parts:

  1. Find a lolo. Customers choose their area and see nearby lolos, with distance, preparation time and a clear Open or Closed label.
  2. Build a plate. Each dish shows its price in US dollars and its options, such as a side, a sauce and a spice level. Dishes that have sold out are marked so that nobody orders them.
  3. Order and pay. Customers order ahead and pay by card, by an in-app wallet or in cash on pick-up, so the app helps people without cash but does not exclude people who prefer it.
  4. Track the order. A simple tracker shows when the order has been received, when it is on the grill and when it is ready or on its way.
  5. Owner mode. The lolo owner sees incoming orders and taps large buttons to accept them, mark them ready and switch dishes to sold out.

Sign-in will use a phone number and a one-time text code, so users do not need to remember a password. For the CAPE Internal Assessment, which asks for a proposal that leads to a working digital solution (Caribbean Examinations Council, 2026), the group will design the app through sketches, wireframes and mock-ups, then build a clickable HTML prototype and test it with real users. A full public launch is outside the scope of the school project but will be costed in the final budget.

Target Audience

The audience is specific, local and split into segments with different needs. Avoid claiming that a product is for “all ages and genders”. Saying who it is not for shows judgement.

LoloGo has three groups of users, and each has different needs:

  • Students and young workers (about 15 to 30). They are phone-first, short on time and often have a fixed break. They need to order ahead and collect quickly.
  • Visitors. Cruise passengers and hotel guests want authentic local food but do not know where lolos are, when they open or what the dishes are. They need location, opening status and clear descriptions, ideally in more than one language, and they often prefer card payment.
  • Lolo owners. Small grill and johnny cake vendors want more orders without a longer line. They need an owner screen that is fast to use with busy hands, with no typing and no complicated settings.

The app is not aimed at large restaurants, which already use delivery platforms. Older customers who prefer to queue and pay cash will still be served at the counter; LoloGo adds an option and does not replace walk-in service.

Inspiration

Personal inspiration is welcome, but a strong answer links it to a wider pattern and to evidence the group will gather. The quote is credited to the member who said it.

“We kept missing johnny cakes before school because the line was too long.”

Candidate B, group member

The idea came from the group’s own mornings. On the way to school, the queue at a favourite lolo was often too long to reach the front before the bell, and by lunchtime the dish we wanted had sometimes sold out. When we talked about it in class, other students described the same experience, and one classmate whose family hosts visitors said that guests often ask where to find “real local food” but give up when they cannot find it.

We noticed that the problem is not the food, which people love, but information and access. Customers cannot see what is available, when, or how to pay. Large delivery apps tend to focus on chain restaurants, not roadside grills. We wanted local vendors to benefit from the digital economy too. The creative design process, which moves from understanding users to testing ideas with them, suits this kind of everyday problem (Brown, 2008).

Collection of Data

This is the section that most often stays vague. A strong plan names each instrument, the sample, the dates, the ethics and how the results will be analysed, and it links every instrument to a question the group needs answered. The school’s sample listed “surveys” and “webinars” without saying what they would find out.

Before designing anything, the group needs evidence that the problem is real and that an app is the right solution. The research questions are: (1) How often do people buy from lolos, and what frustrates them? (2) Which features would make an ordering app worth using? (3) How do owners work at peak times, and what would stop them using an app? The group will use three methods (see the table below), so that what people say can be checked against what they do.

Sampling. The questionnaire will be shared through school and family WhatsApp groups (convenience and snowball sampling). Because this would reach mostly students, the group will also place QR-code posters at the partner lolos to reach visitors and older customers.

Ethics. Taking part is voluntary and anonymous. The questionnaire will not ask for names. Respondents under 18 will need a signed parental consent letter. The interview will be recorded only with written consent, and owners and lolos will be given pseudonyms if they prefer. Observers will not record faces or names. All data will be stored in the school Google Drive and deleted three months after the 2026 results.

Analysis. Closed questions will be summarised as counts and percentages in Google Sheets and presented as pie charts, bar graphs and tables. Open answers and the interview will be grouped into themes. The findings will be used to write the problem statement and objectives, to rank the features in a product backlog, and later to test the prototype with at least five users (Nielsen, 2000).
