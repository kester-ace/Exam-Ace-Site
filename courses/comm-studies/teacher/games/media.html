Old School vs New School Media: Characteristics & Assumptions

Communication Studies Syllabus · 2026-10-12

Course Overview

This two-week unit has students compare traditional and new media by their characteristics and by the assumptions each one carries about audiences, language and culture. It is built on Chapter 6, Technology, Culture and Communication, which the textbook ties to Module 2 and Specific Objectives 5, 7, 8, 9 and 10.

  • Programme: CAPE Communication Studies, Unit 2: Understanding Communication in Society

  • Proposed length: 6 sessions over 2 weeks (adjust to your timetable)

  • Core text: Chapter 6, pp. 76–91, including Activities 6.1–6.8 and the Evaluation and Extension questions

  • Central question: How do the tools we communicate with shape our language, culture and relationships, and who is included or left out when they change?

  • Prior learning: Chapters 4 and 5 on language, Creoles and Standard English

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the unit, students will be able to:

  1. Describe the interactive relationship among communication technologies, language and society.

  2. Trace the shift from print and electromagnetic technologies (telegraph, telephone, radio, television) to internet-based media.

  3. Identify the defining characteristics of traditional and new media and compare them on speed, reach, interactivity, volume and control.

  4. Explain how culture influences language and how technology in turn reshapes both.

  5. Evaluate the assumptions behind claims such as "new media connects everyone" or "traditional media is outdated".

  6. Discuss how differences in culture and access affect integration, marginalisation and alienation in a technology-driven society.

  7. Choose an appropriate medium and register for a given purpose and audience, observing etiquette.

Traditional Media: Characteristics

Traditional media are the print and electronic channels that dominated before the internet: the printing press, handwritten and typed letters, the telegraph, telephone, radio and television. Chapter 6 credits the printing press with the first mass communication vehicle and dates electronic communication from the electromagnet in 1825.

  • Physical and fixed: messages travel as paper, records, cassettes or broadcast signals, and people go to the library, the post office or the television set to receive them.

  • One-to-many, one-way: a publisher, station or network sends and audiences receive, with little immediate reply.

  • Slower and scheduled: a letter reply could take a week or two, and viewers once rushed home for a favourite programme.

  • Gatekept: editors, producers and broadcasters decide what reaches the public, so production is limited to institutions.

  • Formal conventions: letters follow set layout, paragraphing, greetings and closings.

  • Bounded by geography and cost: reach depends on postage, transport, printing and transmission infrastructure.

  • Broadcast influence on culture: the chapter notes how long exposure to North American television changed Caribbean vocabulary, dress and norms.

New Media: Characteristics

New media are digital, internet-based channels such as email, instant messaging, SMS, VOIP, blogs, social networks, streaming and online marketplaces. The chapter's main point is that speed, volume and interactivity change how, when and with whom we communicate.

  • Speed and immediacy: responses arrive in seconds, even across continents, and people can run several conversations at once.

  • Interactive and many-to-many: audiences reply, share and publish through chat rooms, blogs, e-groups and sites such as YouTube.

  • Digital and portable: e-books, downloaded music, streamed shows and online courses reach people on phones and laptops, anywhere.

  • Information overload: large volumes of messages require filtering, discernment and strong reading and listening skills.

  • Informal, compressed language: abbreviations, Netlingo and short email notes replace formal letter conventions, and new words and meanings ("tweet", "surf", "mouse") enter everyday speech.

  • Anonymity and constructed identity: anyone can register an address or build a false profile, which raises privacy, cyber-bullying and trust concerns.

  • Blurred public and private: intimate details and images are shared with strangers, once taboo in most cultures.

  • English-dominant content: most journals and reference material online are in English, though translation tools such as Google Translate help.

  • Commercial reach: online shopping, banking and marketing operate without travel, and post offices report more parcels but fewer letters.

Caribbean Contexts

Students anchor each concept in media they know. The prompts below are suggestions; confirm local details before using them in class.

  • Print: compare a local newspaper (for example, The Gleaner, the Trinidad Guardian or The Daily Herald) with its online presence. What changed in layout, speed and reader comments?

  • Telecommunications: trace how island communication moved from fixed-line telephones to mobile data, messaging apps and VOIP, linking to the chapter's account of the telephone, SMS and Skype.

  • Hurricane Irma (2017): which channels worked when networks failed, and which did people rely on afterwards? Use this to test the assumption that new media is always more reliable.

  • Language: many Caribbean territories, such as Sint Maarten, Curaçao, Suriname and Belize, are multilingual. Discuss how English, Dutch, Spanish, French, Papiamentu and Creoles appear in texts, captions and group chats, linking to the chapter's discussion of migration and language change.

  • Culture and music: connect Activity 6.3 to dancehall, soca and calypso, and how clothing and gestures communicate messages across cultures.

  • Tourism and business: link to the chapter's section on online shopping and marketing, asking how local businesses and the airport and port economy use social media to reach visitors.

  • Access: discuss who on the island has strong connectivity and devices, to examine the digital divide raised in Question 5.

Session-by-Session Content

Session 1: Culture, Language and Communication

  • Objectives: define culture; explain how culture shapes language; recognise how language differences affect business communication.

  • Teach: culture as the common practices and beliefs of a group, expressed through language, proverbs, folk tales, music, food and interaction. Show how Creoles, folklore (the rolling calf, the Bolom, Tata Duhende), carnival names, place names and foods reflect colonisation, migration and heritage. Introduce the three categories of language difficulty in business: gross translation errors, subtle shadings of meaning, and variations within the same language (the "embarazada" slogan example).

  • Activity: Activity 6.1 (list adopted North American words and judge whether they enhance or degrade; trace five local dishes to their source; compare local folklore characters with those of other islands). Activity 6.2 (identify the cultural factors David Victor says cause misunderstanding).

  • Check: exit ticket naming one way culture influenced a word, dish or place name in your territory.

Session 2: Music, Clothing and Body Language

  • Objectives: show that clothing, gesture and music communicate messages; explain how a symbol can change meaning across time and culture.

  • Teach: the 1960s peace sign as a symbol of goodwill that crossed cultures, compared with the dancehall gesture linked to aggression. Discuss how music shapes dress and body language, and why audiences should notice the message in gesture as well as in song.

  • Activity: Activity 6.3 (collect images of dancehall or hip hop wear and comment on the message sent; identify other genres with identifiable clothing and the messages they communicate). Extend with a quick "same gesture, different meaning" gallery walk.

  • Check: students write two sentences explaining how one symbol changed meaning between cultures or decades.

Session 3: Traditional Media and the Evolution of Technology

  • Objectives: trace the development of communication technology; list the characteristics of traditional media; explain interactive communication competencies.

  • Teach: technology as the means people use to improve their surroundings. Timeline: printing press (fifteenth century), electromagnet (1825), telegraph, telephone, radio, television, fax. Link each to how it changed speed and reach. Introduce the NCREL competencies for choosing modes of interaction and for behaving during interaction.

  • Activity: Activity 6.4 (self-rate against the competencies; trace electronic technology from the electromagnet). Students build a class timeline and tag each item as one-way or two-way.

  • Check: students name two traditional media and one characteristic of each, citing the timeline.

Session 4: New Media and Changing Language

  • Objectives: describe the characteristics of new media; explain how technology creates and changes words; adapt language style to medium and audience.

  • Teach: the dominance of English online, new words ("Blogger", "Wiki"), compound words ("homepage", "Facebook"), new meanings for old words ("surf", "mouse", "tweet"), accepted abbreviations (USB, HTML, MP3) and Netlingo. Compare the handwritten letter and the email on greeting, paragraphing, closure and tone, and show how SMS, IM and VOIP replaced slower channels.

  • Activity: Activity 6.5 (group chart of internet and digital words, and a list of technology abbreviations with their meanings). Activity 6.6 (rewrite the instant message conversation in Standard English). Then students convert the formal letter into an email and back.

  • Check: students explain in one sentence when Netlingo is appropriate and when it is not.

Session 5: New Media in Daily Life and the Assumptions Test

  • Objectives: analyse benefits and risks of new media in reading, listening, learning, social life and business; test assumptions about new media.

  • Teach: e-books and e-readers, downloaded and streamed music and television, recorded lectures (with permission), online courses, chat rooms, blogs, YouTube, online shopping and banking. Use the Rosen article to weigh social networking's benefits (virtual empathy, practice socialising for introverts, teaching tools) against its risks (distraction, narcissistic tendencies, overuse). Introduce privacy, anonymity, false identities and cyber-bullying.

  • Activity: Activity 6.7 (how social networking could be an innovative teaching tool; whether parents should monitor children's activity and why; how people will communicate in ten years). Run a four-corners debate on one assumption from the table above.

  • Check: students name one assumption they now question and the evidence that changed their mind.

Session 6: Critique and Consolidation

  • Objectives: discuss how culture and access affect integration, marginalisation and alienation; synthesise the unit.

  • Teach: information overload and the need for discernment; the risk that internet culture mainly represents dominant, young and middle-class groups; the argument that interpersonal skills may weaken when communication moves online; the chapter's conclusion that effective communication means choosing the method and learning its protocol.

  • Activity: Activity 6.8 (compare technology's effect on your life now and ten years ago; discuss trends that may affect your culture). Plan the extended response on Evaluation and Extension Question 5, with a paragraph outline built in pairs.

  • Check: students submit the paragraph outline, and teams complete the assumptions debate if not already done.
