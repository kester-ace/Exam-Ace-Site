Data Hunters

2026-10-13

CAPE Communication Studies, Module 1: Gathering, Organising and Processing Information. Objective 9: use the appropriate data collection methods and instruments for research purposes.

Overview

Research only works when the data is gathered with a method that fits the question. Objective 9 asks you to choose and use the right method (the way data is collected) and the right instrument (the tool that records it).

Primary data is collected first-hand by the researcher. Secondary data already exists, such as reports or records. Five methods are covered in this objective:

MethodInstrumentData type
InterviewsInterview schedulePrimary
Focus groupsFocus group schedulePrimary
SurveysQuestionnairePrimary
ObservationObservation schedule or checklistPrimary
Document analysisDocument analysis guideSecondary

Key terms

  • Respondent: a person who answers questions in a study.

  • Sample: the group of people chosen to represent the whole population.

  • Qualitative data: descriptive data in words, such as opinions and experiences.

  • Quantitative data: numerical data that can be counted and measured.

  • Bias: anything that pushes results away from the truth, such as a leading question.

1. Interviews

An interview is a planned conversation in which the researcher asks questions and the respondent answers. It gives rich, detailed qualitative data because the researcher can probe and clarify.

Types by delivery

TypeHow it worksBest used when
Face to faceResearcher and respondent meet in personDetail and body language matter
TelephoneQuestions asked by phoneRespondents are far apart or busy
OnlineVideo call or live chat, such as Teams or ZoomRespondents are overseas or cannot travel

Types by structure

  • Structured: the same fixed questions in the same order for every respondent. Easy to compare.

  • Semi-structured: a set of main questions with freedom to probe further. The most common type in research.

  • Unstructured: an open conversation guided by a topic only. Flexible but hard to compare.

The interview schedule

The interview schedule is the written list of questions and prompts the interviewer follows. It usually contains:

  1. Title, date, time, place and interviewer's name

  2. Respondent's code or name (with consent)

  3. A short introduction stating the purpose and promising confidentiality

  4. Warm-up questions, then the main questions, then closing questions

  5. Prompts and probes, such as "Can you give an example?"

  6. Space to record answers or notes

  7. A closing thank-you

Strengths

  • Detailed, in-depth answers

  • The interviewer can clarify and probe

  • High response rate compared with mailed questionnaires

  • Suitable for people who cannot read or write well

Limitations

  • Time-consuming and costly to conduct and transcribe

  • Interviewer bias, such as tone or reactions, can influence answers

  • Small samples, so results are hard to generalise

  • Respondents may give answers they think are expected

Caribbean example: A researcher studying how hurricane recovery affected small businesses in Roseau, Dominica, interviews five business owners using a semi-structured schedule and records the interviews with their permission.

2. Focus Groups

A focus group is a guided discussion with a small group of people, usually 6 to 10, who share a common characteristic related to the topic. A moderator leads the discussion while a note-taker or recording device captures what is said.

The researcher is interested in how opinions form and change as participants react to one another, not only in individual answers.

Roles

  • Moderator: introduces the topic, keeps the discussion on track, makes sure everyone speaks, and stays neutral.

  • Note-taker: records key points, quotes and group dynamics.

  • Participants: share views openly and respect others' turns to speak.

The focus group schedule

The focus group schedule (also called a discussion guide) outlines the flow of the session. It usually includes:

  1. Welcome, purpose of the study, ground rules and confidentiality

  2. An icebreaker so participants feel comfortable

  3. Main discussion questions, ordered from general to specific

  4. Probes and follow-up prompts

  5. A closing round where each person adds a final thought

  6. Thanks and a reminder of how the findings will be used

Strengths

  • Collects many views quickly

  • Group interaction can reveal ideas that individual interviews miss

  • Useful for testing a product, message or idea before launch

Limitations

  • Dominant speakers can silence others

  • Participants may follow the group instead of stating their true opinion (groupthink)

  • Sensitive topics may not be shared openly

  • Results cannot be generalised to a whole population

Caribbean example: A media company in Guyana holds a focus group with eight secondary students to learn which social media platforms they use for news before redesigning its website.

3. Surveys and Questionnaires

A survey is a method of collecting data from a large number of people. The most common instrument is the questionnaire, a written set of questions that respondents answer themselves or that a researcher reads out. Surveys produce mainly quantitative data that can be counted and shown in tables and charts.

Ways of administering a survey

ModeHow it worksMain advantageMain drawback
OnlineLink sent by email, social media or an app such as Google FormsFast, cheap, easy to analyseExcludes people without internet access
Face to faceResearcher hands out or reads the questionnaire in personHigh response rate, can explain questionsTime-consuming, interviewer presence may influence answers
TelephoneQuestions asked by phoneReaches scattered respondents quicklyCalls may be ignored, limited length
MailPaper questionnaire posted with a return envelopeReaches people without internet, no interviewer biasLow response rate, slow

Types of questions

  • Closed-ended: fixed answers to choose from, such as yes/no, multiple choice, rating scales (for example, a Likert scale from Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree) or ranking. Easy to analyse.

  • Open-ended: respondents answer in their own words. Rich in detail but harder to analyse.

  • Contingency (filter) questions: answered only if a previous answer applies, such as "If yes, how often?"

Rules for good questionnaire design

  1. Use clear, simple language the respondents understand.

  2. Ask one thing per question. Avoid double-barrelled questions such as "Is the service fast and friendly?"

  3. Avoid leading questions, such as "Don't you agree that...?"

  4. Avoid vague or sensitive wording, and keep personal questions to the end.

  5. Order questions logically, from easy to difficult.

  6. Give a cover note stating the purpose, confidentiality and how to return the form.

  7. Provide clear instructions and enough space for answers.

  8. Pilot test the questionnaire on a few people before using it widely.

Strengths

  • Collects data from many people at low cost

  • Anonymity encourages honest answers

  • Standard questions make results easy to compare and analyse statistically

Limitations

  • Respondents may misunderstand questions and cannot ask for help

  • Low response rates, especially for mailed and online forms

  • Answers may be careless, incomplete or untruthful

  • Little depth compared with interviews

Caribbean example: A student in Belize surveys 100 students at Calabash Point Secondary and Sandbox Tree College on mobile data spending, using an online questionnaire with closed-ended and Likert scale questions.

4. Observation

Observation is the method of watching and recording people's behaviour, events or conditions as they happen, instead of asking about them. It shows what people actually do, which can differ from what they say they do.

Types of observation

  • Participant observation: the researcher joins the group being studied and takes part in its activities.

  • Non-participant observation: the researcher watches from outside without taking part.

  • Overt observation: the people know they are being observed.

  • Covert observation: the people do not know. This raises ethical issues and needs careful justification.

  • Structured observation: behaviour is recorded using a prepared schedule or checklist.

  • Unstructured observation: the researcher records whatever seems relevant in field notes.

Observation schedule and checklist

An observation schedule is a planned form for recording what is seen, usually at set times or intervals. An observation checklist is a simpler list of behaviours or items that the researcher ticks off as they occur.

A good schedule or checklist contains:

  1. Date, time, location and name of the observer

  2. The purpose of the observation

  3. A list of specific, observable behaviours or items to look for

  4. Space to tick, tally or note how often and how long each occurs

  5. A comments section for unexpected events

Sample observation checklist: customer service at a Kingstown shop

Behaviour observedYesNoNotes
Customer greeted within one minute
Staff member makes eye contact
Product information explained
Payment processed correctly
Customer thanked on leaving

Strengths

  • Records real behaviour as it happens

  • Does not depend on respondents' memory or honesty

  • Useful for people who cannot be interviewed, such as young children

Limitations

  • Time-consuming and cannot explain why people behave as they do

  • Hawthorne effect: people may change their behaviour when they know they are being watched

  • Observer bias, because two observers may interpret the same event differently

  • Ethical concerns about privacy and consent

5. Document Analysis

Document analysis is the systematic study of existing written, visual or recorded material to answer a research question. The researcher does not collect new data from people. The documents are secondary sources that already exist.

Sources of documents

  • Public records: government reports, census data, laws, policies, minutes of meetings

  • Media: newspapers, magazines, websites, radio and television broadcasts, social media posts

  • Organisational documents: annual reports, memos, emails, brochures, school records

  • Personal documents: diaries, letters, photographs, journals

  • Archives and statistics: historical records, published statistics, previous research studies

Steps in document analysis

  1. State the research question the documents must help answer.

  2. Locate and select relevant documents.

  3. Check the document's authenticity, credibility, representativeness and meaning.

  4. Read closely and record key points, quotes and patterns.

  5. Code or group the information into themes or categories.

  6. Interpret the findings and relate them to the research question.

Evaluating a document

CriterionQuestion to ask
AuthenticityIs it genuine and what it claims to be?
CredibilityIs the author or source trustworthy and free of serious error?
RepresentativenessIs it typical of its kind, or an unusual example?
MeaningIs the content clear, and what was it written to achieve?
CurrencyIs it recent enough for the research purpose?

Strengths

  • Low cost and easy to access

  • Non-reactive, because the documents are not affected by being studied

  • Allows study of past events and long-term change

Limitations

  • Documents may be biased, incomplete or written for a different purpose

  • Some documents are confidential or hard to obtain

  • Cannot be questioned to explain what they mean

Caribbean example: A researcher compares how The Daily Herald reported on Hurricane Irma recovery over several years by analysing articles for key themes.

Choosing the Right Method

Choose the method by asking what you need to know, from whom, and how much time and money you have. Many studies combine methods, which is called triangulation and makes findings more reliable.

MethodData producedSample sizeTime and costBest for
InterviewQualitative, in depthSmallHighPersonal experiences and detailed opinions
Focus groupQualitative, group viewsSmall groupsMediumTesting ideas and exploring attitudes
Survey / questionnaireMainly quantitativeLargeLowMeasuring opinions or habits across many people
ObservationQualitative or quantitativeSmall to mediumHighReal behaviour and events
Document analysisQualitative or quantitativeDepends on documentsLowPast events, trends and official positions

Ethical research checklist

  • Obtain informed consent before collecting data.

  • Protect confidentiality and anonymity.

  • Explain the purpose honestly and allow people to withdraw.

  • Store data securely and report findings accurately.

  • Acknowledge all sources used.

Review Questions

  1. Distinguish between a method and an instrument of data collection, giving one example of each.

  2. Describe three ways in which an interview can be conducted.

  3. State two advantages and two disadvantages of using focus groups.

  4. What is an interview schedule, and what should it contain?

  5. Explain the difference between closed-ended and open-ended questions. Give one example of each.

  6. Identify three problems to avoid when writing questionnaire items.

  7. A researcher wants to find out how students use their lunch break at school. Justify why observation would be suitable, and name one limitation of the method.

  8. Explain what is meant by the Hawthorne effect.

  9. List four sources of documents a researcher could analyse, and state how the researcher can judge whether a document is credible.

  10. A researcher wishes to study public opinion on a new tourism policy in The Bahamas. Recommend two data collection methods and justify your choices.

Suggested answers

  1. A method is the way data is gathered, such as a survey. An instrument is the tool used to record it, such as a questionnaire.

  2. Face to face, by telephone and online (video call or live chat).

  3. Advantages: many views are collected quickly, and group interaction reveals new ideas. Disadvantages: dominant members may silence others, and participants may follow the group (groupthink).

  4. It is the written list of questions and prompts the interviewer follows. It contains an introduction, ordered questions, probes, space for answers and a closing.

  5. Closed-ended questions have fixed answers, such as "Do you own a smartphone? Yes / No". Open-ended questions let respondents answer freely, such as "Why do you prefer this brand?"

  6. Any three of: leading questions, double-barrelled questions, vague wording, difficult language, sensitive questions placed early, illogical order.

  7. Observation shows actual behaviour rather than reported behaviour. Limitation: it is time-consuming, or people may change their behaviour when watched.

  8. People alter their behaviour because they know they are being observed.

  9. Any four of: government reports, newspapers, websites, annual reports, diaries, minutes, statistics. Credibility is judged by checking the author, source, date, accuracy and possible bias.

  10. A survey would gather views from many residents, and interviews with officials or business owners would add depth. Combining the two (triangulation) makes the findings more reliable.
