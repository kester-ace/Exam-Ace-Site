Prove It or Move It

2026-09-30

Overview

Both forms aim to convince a reader, but argumentative writing wins by proving a claim, while persuasive writing wins by moving the reader to believe or act.

Argumentative writing

Argumentative writing builds a case through logic and evidence. The writer states a claim, supports it with facts, data and reasoning, and addresses counterarguments fairly, often conceding valid points before rebutting them.

The reader is treated as someone who will weigh the evidence and decide on the merits. The tone is measured and objective.

Persuasive writing

Persuasive writing aims to move the reader to believe something or take action, using whatever works: emotional appeals, personal anecdotes, vivid language and the writer's own credibility.

It may mention opposing views, but it doesn't need to engage them in depth. The tone is often more personal and urgent.

Same topic, two approaches: school uniforms

Argumentative: "Studies of schools that adopted uniform policies show mixed results on academic performance, but consistent reductions in reported bullying related to clothing. While critics argue uniforms limit self-expression, this concern is outweighed by..."

Persuasive: "Imagine a child who dreads the morning because of what she'll wear. Uniforms end that worry. Every student walks in as an equal. Support uniforms for your school today."

In practice

The line between the two often blurs. Strong pieces combine an evidence-based argument with enough emotional resonance to land.

In some curricula, particularly Caribbean and IB contexts, the terms overlap, with persuasion treated as a set of techniques used within argument. When teaching or writing for a specific syllabus, check how that exam body defines each.
