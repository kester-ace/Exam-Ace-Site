Communication Studies Assignment: Non-Communicable Diseases in Children

2026-10-08

Students research non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in children, write a speech or short story for parents, then justify how it is organized and evaluate how well it works.

CAPE Communication Studies

The task

You will research non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in children, turn that research into one persuasive piece for parents, and then explain and judge your own work. The assignment has four parts.

PartWhat you submitLength
A. Research notesDefinition, examples, classification, causes, risk factors, prevention and at least six factual statistics, each with a credible source1 page of notes plus a reference list
B. Creative pieceEither a speech (to be delivered to a parent meeting) or a short storySpeech: 550–700 words (about 4–5 minutes). Story: 700–900 words
C. Justification of organizationWhy you ordered and structured the piece the way you did250–300 words
D. Evaluation of effectivenessHow well the piece is likely to work on parents, judged against evidence300–350 words

Audience: parents and guardians of children aged 5–17 in your community. They are busy, they care deeply, and many may feel blamed when they hear about childhood illness. Your piece should inform and motivate without shaming.

Purpose: to help parents understand what NCDs are, how common they are in children, which risk factors they can influence, and what small, realistic steps they can take.

Requirements for Part B

  • Use at least four different literary techniques and label them in your Part C.

  • Include at least four statistics from your research, each woven into the piece, not listed.

  • Name the source of each statistic in the piece ("UNICEF reports…") so parents can trust it.

  • Be accurate: do not suggest that every NCD is preventable or caused by parenting.

  • End with a clear, realistic call to action.

ClassificationGroup the examplesGroup them by body system: metabolic (type 1 and type 2 diabetes, obesity), respiratory (asthma), cancers, cardiovascular (heart disease), and mental health. Confirm the grouping against WHO's NCD fact sheet and say which grouping you used.
CausesWhy children develop NCDsCauses combine genetics and biology with behaviour and environment. Risk factorsWhat raises the chanceWHO names physical inactivity, unhealthy diets, exposure to alcohol and tobacco, and unhealthy or unsafe environments as important risk factors (WHO). PreventionWhat families, schools and governments can doWHO says life-long health behaviours are shaped in childhood and adolescence, and that schools and educators have a responsibility to protect children's health (WHO). The Caribbean Public Health Agency (CARPHA) recommends mandatory food labelling, nutrition standards for schools and less marketing of unhealthy foods (CARPHA). At home: more active play, fewer sugary drinks, no smoking around children and support for children to talk about their feelings.

Part B: Writing your piece for parents

Choose one form. Both must carry the same research and the same call to action.

  • Speech: written to be heard at a parent-teacher meeting. Use short sentences, repetition and direct address ("you", "your child"). Include delivery notes in brackets if they help (pause, slow down).

  • Short story: told through one character, such as a parent noticing a change in their child, a child at a school canteen, or a nurse at a clinic. Weave the facts into dialogue, thought or setting. Do not stop the story to lecture.

Choose at least four techniques from the menu below. Use each one on purpose and be ready to explain it in Part C.

Tone checklist for this audience

  • Inform and encourage; never blame. Some NCDs, such as type 1 diabetes and childhood cancers, are not caused by anything a parent did.

  • Use plain language. Explain "non-communicable" in your first minute.

  • Offer small, realistic actions, not a complete lifestyle change.

  • Respect families who face cost, time or safety barriers to healthy choices.

Parts C and D: Justify and evaluate

Part C: Justification of organization (250–300 words)

Explain why your piece is built the way it is. Address each prompt with evidence from your own text.

  1. Opening: Why did you begin as you did, and what did you want parents to feel or do in the first 30 seconds?

  2. Sequence: Why is the information in this order (for example, scene, then definition, then statistics, then causes, then action)? What would be lost if you swapped two sections?

  3. Techniques: Name each literary technique, quote where it appears and say what it does for this audience.

  4. Language and tone: Which word choices, sentence lengths and forms of address suit parents, and why?

  5. Closing: How does your ending leave the audience with a clear next step?

Part D: Evaluation of effectiveness (300–350 words)

Judge how well your piece is likely to work. A good evaluation weighs strengths against weaknesses and gives reasons.

  1. Purpose and audience: Does the piece inform, persuade and motivate parents without blaming them? Give two pieces of evidence from your text.

  2. Evidence: Are the statistics accurate, current, sourced and easy to understand? Which would you update or double-check?

  3. Feedback: Read or tell your piece to at least one parent or guardian and record two specific comments they made.

  4. Weaknesses: Name one thing that does not work as well as you hoped (too many figures, a confusing section, a missing barrier such as cost) and explain why.

  5. Revision: State two changes you would make, and say how each would improve the effect on parents.

Model speech for teachers (not for copying)

This short exemplar shows the standard expected. It is about 400 words, below the student minimum, so students must go further in depth and in their own voice.

Small Plates, Big Futures

Good evening, parents.

Picture your child at nine years old, running home from school, backpack bouncing, calling out the first thing she wants: a cold drink and a bag of chips. It feels like the smallest moment of the day. But the smallest moments are where the biggest health stories begin.

Tonight I want to talk about non-communicable diseases, or NCDs. These are long-lasting conditions, such as asthma, diabetes, heart disease, cancer, obesity and mental health conditions, that cannot be passed from one child to another like a cold. We think of them as grown-up problems. They are not. The World Health Organization says many of them have their origins in childhood.

Consider the numbers. UNICEF reports that one in ten children aged five to nineteen now lives with obesity, about 188 million young people, and for the first time that is more than the number who are underweight. Here in the Caribbean, the regional health agency CARPHA says one in three children is overweight or obese. Worldwide, 81 percent of adolescents do not get the hour of daily activity they need. And one in seven young people aged ten to nineteen lives with a mental health condition.

Why? Not because parents do not care. Not because parents do not try. But because the world around our children has changed. Ultra-processed food is everywhere. Screens are easier than playgrounds. In a UNICEF poll of 64,000 young people, three in four had seen an advertisement for sugary drinks, snacks or fast food in a single week. Your child is being marketed to, every day.

Now, not every NCD can be prevented. Type 1 diabetes and childhood cancers are not caused by anything a parent did. But many risk factors can be changed: what is on the plate, how much our children move, whether they breathe smoke, and whether they feel safe to say how they really feel.

So here is my invitation. Tonight, choose three small changes. Water before soda. Sixty minutes of movement a day. And one question at the dinner table: "How are you really feeling?"

Small plates. Big futures. The habits our children form today will walk beside them for the rest of their lives. Let us walk with them.
