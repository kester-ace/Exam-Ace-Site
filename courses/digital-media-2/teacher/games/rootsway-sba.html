1Proposal

1.1Introduction

Overview: brief introduction (background) · 1/1. Criterion: Comprehensive: provides clear, relevant context for the project.Why it earned this: It sets the local problem (heritage known by few, elders’ memories at risk), uses a credible secondary source and the group’s own figures, and ends with the product in one sentence.To reach full marks: Already at full marks. Keep every statistic traceable to Section 4 or the appendices, as here.

Sint Maarten’s history is all around us: the Great Salt Pond, the old courthouse on Front Street, Fort Amsterdam and the hillside paths of Cul de Sac. Yet most of it lives in the memories of elders. UNESCO (2003) warns that oral heritage disappears when it is not recorded and passed on.

Our questionnaire of 72 people (Section 4.3) found that 51.4% know little or nothing about local history and 68.1% had not visited a heritage site in the past year. Rootsway SXM closes that gap with trails people can walk, hear and book.

1.2Description of Service

Description of the product or service · 3/3. Criterion: Clear rationale and purpose (2) and a precisely identified target audience (1).Why it earned this: The service is described feature by feature, the purpose is stated separately (1.3) with evidence for each feature in Table 1, and the audience is defined by age, role and situation (1.4).To reach full marks: Already at full marks. A list of pages with no reason for each one would earn 1 for rationale.

Rootsway SXM is a free, mobile-friendly heritage website. Visitors choose a self-guided trail, follow a map with numbered stops and press play at each stop to hear an elder’s short audio or video story, with transcripts in English, Spanish and Dutch. It also offers an events calendar, a booking form for guided school walks and free teacher resources. It runs on WordPress, designed from a hand-coded home page.

1.3Purpose of Service

The purpose of Rootsway SXM is to make local heritage easy to experience on foot and to preserve elders’ memories before they are lost. Table 1 links each feature to the evidence behind it.

1.4Target Audience

The primary audience is secondary students aged 12 to 19 and their Social Studies and History teachers. The secondary audience is residents and families aged 20 to 65, and visitors who want more than the beach. As 70.8% of respondents would mostly use a phone, the site is mobile first.

1.5Concept

Concept: how the idea works · 1/1. Criterion: Clearly articulated: the reader understands exactly how the product works.Why it earned this: Five steps take the reader from the home page to a booked walk, the funding model is stated, and the colour palette is explained as part of the concept.To reach full marks: Already at full marks.

Rootsway SXM works in five steps:

  1. The visitor opens the site; no sign-up is needed.
  2. They choose a trail and see its route, time and access notes.
  3. At each stop they play a story; the map route draws on to the next stop.
  4. They check the calendar for guided walks.
  5. A teacher books a school walk and receives an emailed reference.

Everything is free to users; a grant, sponsors, donations and paid adult walks fund it.

The palette (Figure 1) comes from the trails: teal for Philipsburg’s shutters and sea (navigation), hill green for Cul de Sac, sun amber for booking, brick red for old roofs (stories) and sand for the salt pond (backgrounds).

1.6Significance of Concept

Concept: significance · 2/2. Criterion: At least two well-supported justifications for the significance of the concept.Why it earned this: Three justifications (preserving oral heritage, active learning for schools, local pride and small-business benefit), each supported by evidence.To reach full marks: Already at full marks. One justification would earn 1.

The concept matters for three reasons. It preserves oral heritage: every story recorded now is not lost. It makes history active; our interviewee said students “remember what they stood on” (Appendix A.2). And it builds pride while spreading visitors beyond the cruise-ship streets to small businesses on the trails.

3.5User Experience Testing

Appropriate user experience · 1/2. Criterion: Clear navigation, feedback and accessibility suited to the target audience.Why it earned this: Navigation and audio feedback test well, but only 6 of 10 testers could book a school walk on a phone: the date picker and the dropdowns are hard to use on small screens, and the form gives no error message when a weekend date is chosen.To reach full marks: Replace the date picker with a mobile-friendly date field, show clear error messages beside each field, and retest the booking task on two phone sizes before submission.

Ten target users aged 15 to 67 tested v1.0 (13 to 17 April 2026), completing 44 of 50 tasks (88.0%, Table 13) and rating each interactive design principle from 1 to 5 (Figure 32, Table 12).

  • Perceivability (4.0): play buttons stood out; phone transcripts were too small for two older testers.
  • Learnability (4.7): after one stop, everyone played the next unaided.
  • Predictability (4.4): map numbers matched the stop list.
  • Feedback (3.7): lowest; the form gave no message when a weekend date was rejected.

4.3Data Analysis

Research: data to justify the project · 3/3. Criterion: Data capture instrument designed with data collected (1) and a thorough data analysis (2).Why it earned this: Both instruments are designed and justified, 72 responses are charted, and the analysis compares groups, links every finding to a design decision and cross-checks the interview.To reach full marks: Already at full marks. Describing each chart without saying what it means for the product would earn 2.

The sample included students (41.7%), parents and residents (25.0%), teachers (19.4%) and tourism or heritage workers (13.9%) (Figure 35), so every target group is represented.

Knowledge is low: 37 of 72 (51.4%) know little or nothing about local history and only 8 (11.1%) know a lot (Figure 36); 49 (68.1%) made no heritage visit last year. This confirmed the problem.

Respondents prefer short audio or video on a phone (26, 36.1%) or walking with a guide (21, 29.2%) (Figure 37): 47 (65.3%) favour exactly what a story trail offers, against 9 (12.5%) for plaques. Phones lead (51, 70.8%), so the site is mobile first.

Trail maps (54, 75.0%), elders’ stories (49, 68.1%) and events (31, 43.1%) were most wanted (Figure 38), so they lead the home page. Booking ranked fourth overall (28) but was chosen by 13 of 14 teachers; 11 would book and 3 might (Figure 40), so booking sits in the menu. 50 (69.4%) would use the site; 5 (6.9%) would not.

The interview explains the figures. Trips fail on “paperwork and planning, not interest”, which justifies the booking form and risk template; students “remember what they stood on”, which favours trails over a podcast; elders “will not be here forever”, so stories were recorded first. The sample of 72 is small and self-selected, so it shows a trend only.

A.2Interview Guide and Transcript

Interview with Mr. Desmond Arrindell, retired secondary history teacher, 22 October 2025, on his porch in Philipsburg. About 30 minutes, recorded with consent. Interviewers: S. Peterson (questions), M. Hodge (notes). Lightly edited for length.

Q1. How long did you teach history, and how often did you take classes out of school?
Thirty-one years. Maybe twice a year, when I could find the time and the bus money.

Q2. What stops teachers from organising heritage walks?
Paperwork and planning, not interest. Permission slips, a risk sheet, a guide, transport. By the time you do all that, the term is over.

Q3. What do students remember from a walk that they forget from a lesson?
They remember what they stood on. Put a child where the salt was raked and they understand the trade in a way no chapter can teach.

Q4. Which places in Philipsburg would you include, and why?
The pond lookout, Back Street, the courthouse square, Front Street and the boardwalk. You can walk it in an hour and tell three hundred years.

Q5. How should elders’ stories be recorded and shared respectfully?
Ask first, record them where they are comfortable, and let them hear it before you publish. And write their names properly. Our elders will not be here forever.

Q6. What would a teacher need from a website before booking a walk?
A clear route, how long it takes, whether a wheelchair can manage it, a lesson plan and a quick answer. Teachers will not wait two weeks.

Q7. What safety or access issues should we plan for?
Traffic on Front Street on cruise days, shade, water, and steps on the hillside paths. Mark which stops have no steps.

Q8. Is there anything else we should consider?
Keep the stories short. Two minutes. Let the place do the rest.
