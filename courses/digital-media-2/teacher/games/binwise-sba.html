1Proposal

1.1Brief Overview

Overview: brief introduction (background) · 1/1. Criterion: Comprehensive: provides clear, relevant context for the project.Why it earned this: Local context (one landfill, dumping in named districts), a credible secondary source and the group’s own survey figures, all in two short paragraphs.To reach full marks: Already at full marks. Keep every figure traceable to the appendix, as here.

Belize City is low-lying with little spare land, and its rubbish is trucked to one regional landfill at Mile 24 on the George Price Highway. Small and coastal states face particular waste pressures because land for disposal is scarce (Kaza et al., 2018), so every bag collected on time and every bottle recycled matters. In our own neighbourhoods we saw bins put out on the wrong day, bags torn open by dogs, and old fridges and tyres dumped in gutters and empty lots.

Our questionnaire of 64 residents (Appendix 4.2) found that 71.9% had missed a collection at least once because they were unsure of the day, and 76.6% had seen illegal dumping in the last three months. Most residents learn their day from neighbours, which is unreliable. BinWise Belize was designed to close this information gap.

1.2Description of Service

Description of the product or service · 3/3. Criterion: Clear rationale and purpose (2) and a precisely identified target audience (1).Why it earned this: The purpose is stated in one sentence (1.3), every feature is tied to survey evidence in Table 1, and the audience is defined by age, district and situation (1.4).To reach full marks: Already at full marks. Weaker scripts list features without saying why each one is needed.

BinWise Belize is a free mobile app for Android and iPhone. After choosing their district, residents see their next household-garbage and bulky-waste days, get a reminder the evening before, find the nearest recycling drop-off point on a map, and report illegal dumping with a photo and a GPS pin in under a minute. Reports appear on a shared map that community clean-up volunteers use to plan clean-ups. The app is available in English, Spanish and Kriol. Collection days in the prototype are sample data; the live app would load the official published schedule.

1.3Rationale and Key Features

The purpose of BinWise Belize is to replace a confusing, word-of-mouth system with one clear source of information, so that fewer bins go out on the wrong day and dumped waste is cleared sooner. Table 1 links each key feature to the evidence behind it.

1.4Target Audience

The primary audience is householders aged 16 to 65 in the districts shown in Figure 23, especially renters and people who have recently moved, who are least likely to know their day. Smartphone ownership is high: 96.9% of respondents own one. The secondary audiences are community clean-up volunteers, who use the report map, and recycling businesses, who sponsor drop-off listings.

1.5Concept

Concept: how the idea works · 1/1. Criterion: Clearly articulated: the reader understands exactly how the product works.Why it earned this: A five-step walkthrough from first launch to a cleared report, plus the revenue model and the data source, with the ecosystem in Figure 1.To reach full marks: Already at full marks.

BinWise Belize works in five steps:

  1. The user chooses a district once; the app remembers it.
  2. Home reads that district’s schedule from the Adalo database and shows the next collection.
  3. Push notifications remind the user before each collection.
  4. The Map tab lists drop-off points by material, with directions.
  5. The Report tab opens the camera, adds a GPS pin and a category, and posts the report to the shared map, where volunteers move it from Sent to Seen to Cleared.

Residents never pay. Income comes from recycling businesses that sponsor drop-off listings.

1.6Significance of Concept

Concept: significance · 2/2. Criterion: At least two well-supported justifications for the significance of the concept.Why it earned this: Three justifications (landfill pressure, public health, community action), each supported by survey or interview evidence.To reach full marks: Already at full marks. One justification alone would earn 1.

The concept matters for three reasons. First, it eases landfill pressure: a drop-off map makes recycling easier, and 68.8% of respondents said they would recycle more if they knew where to go. Second, it protects public health: dumped tyres hold rainwater where mosquitoes breed, and torn bags attract rats. Third, it turns residents into partners, because every report gives volunteers a map of where to clean next, a need the interviewee stressed (Appendix 4.1.2).

2.1Methodology

Research: data to justify the project · 3/3. Criterion: Data capture instrument designed with data collected (1) and a thorough data analysis (2).Why it earned this: Two instruments (questionnaire, interview) are designed and justified here; the instruments, a completed response, five charts and an analysis that cross-checks both sources are in Appendix 4.To reach full marks: Already at full marks. Charts with no written analysis would earn 2 at most.

The group used a questionnaire and an interview (Table 8). The 12-question Google Forms questionnaire ran from 20 October to 7 November 2025 and was shared through WhatsApp groups and QR flyers at school; 64 residents answered. A 25-minute interview with a community clean-up volunteer on 28 October 2025 explained how dumping is found and cleared. Both used informed consent, and under-18s had parental consent.

Table 8. Data capture instruments: benefits and limitations
InstrumentTypeBenefitsLimitations and how we reduced them
Online questionnaire (n = 64)QuantitativeCheap, quick, reaches many districts; responses compiled and charted automaticallyOnly reaches phone users and may be skewed by who chooses to answer; QR flyers and family networks widened the sample
Semi-structured interview (1 volunteer)QualitativeRich detail and follow-up questions; expert view of how dumping is clearedOne person’s view; scheduling took two weeks; checked against survey data

For the design, we followed the class handout’s three stages (Small, 2026). Paper low-fidelity wireframes fixed layout and user flow cheaply; Balsamiq mid-fidelity frames tested hierarchy; Figma mock-ups then added colour and type; and a clickable Figma prototype let users test the flow before we built the working app in Adalo. Each stage added detail only after the previous one was approved.

3.4User Experience Testing

Appropriate user experience · 2/2. Criterion: Clear navigation, feedback and accessibility suited to the target audience.Why it earned this: Eight target users completed 37 of 40 tasks (92.5%); ratings are mapped to the five interactive design principles in Table 16 and Figure 22, each with evidence and a next step.To reach full marks: Already at full marks.

Eight residents aged 16 to 61 from six districts tested v1.0 from 13 to 17 April 2026. Each completed five tasks (Table 17) and rated the app from 1 to 5 on each interactive design principle (Figure 22). They completed 37 of 40 tasks (92.5%).

  • Consistency (4.6): the same header, tab bar and colour meanings appear on every screen, so testers moved between tabs without help.
  • Perceivability (4.4): the coral Report tab was easy to notice, but one tester missed the bulky-waste card below the fold.
  • Learnability (4.8): the highest score; first-time users reported dumping in a mean of 48 seconds.
  • Predictability (4.3): the lowest score; two testers expected the map pins to open details on one tap, not two.
  • Feedback (4.5): the reminder toast and the Sent, Seen, Cleared status told users what had happened.

For v1.1 the group will open pin details on one tap and split the Report screen into two steps.

4.1.2Interview Transcript

Interview with Ms. Marisol Arana, volunteer with a Collet clean-up group, 28 October 2025, at a community centre in Collet. Lightly edited for length.

Q1. How long have you volunteered with clean-ups, and in which areas?
About six years, mostly Collet and Southside, sometimes Port Loyola when they need hands.

Q2. What kinds of illegal dumping do you see most often?
Fridges, stoves, mattresses, tyres, and building rubble after people renovate. Tyres are the worst: after a rain they hold water and you get mosquitoes.

Q3. How do you find out where dumping has happened?
Mostly somebody sends a photo in a WhatsApp group, but there is no location, so we are driving around looking for it. Half the time we never find the spot.

Q4. Why do you think people dump instead of waiting for bulky-waste collection?
Some people don’t know there is a bulky-waste day, or they miss it and don’t want a fridge in the yard for weeks. Some just don’t care, but most people I meet would do right if it was easy.

Q5. Do residents know their collection days? How can you tell?
Plenty don’t. You see bags out on the wrong day and the dogs get them. People who rent or just moved here are the ones who ask me most.

Q6. What problems does dumped waste cause in the neighbourhood?
Smell, rats, mosquitoes, and it looks bad. When the rain comes it washes into the gutters and down to the pond and the sea.

Q7. Would a photo report with a map pin help your group? What information would you need?
Yes, if it has the exact spot and a photo, and what kind of waste, so we know if we need a truck or just bags. And a way to mark it done, so people see something happened.

Q8. Which languages would an app need to reach everyone?
English for sure, Spanish, and Kriol. A lot of our older residents speak Spanish at home.

Q9. Is there anything else we should consider?
Keep it simple. If it takes more than a minute, people won’t bother.

4.3Data Analysis

The sample covered all six district groups (Figure 23), with Northside the largest (21.9%) and Belama and Bella Vista the smallest (12.5%), so no single area dominates the results. Almost every respondent owns a smartphone (96.9%), which supports delivering the solution as an app.

Figure 24 shows that the largest group, 26 of 64 (40.6%), learn their collection day from neighbours, and a further 12 (18.8%) do not know it at all. Only 9 (14.1%) use radio or newspapers. Together, 38 respondents (59.4%) depend on word of mouth or have no source, which explains Figure 25: 19 (29.7%) have missed a collection often and 27 (42.2%) sometimes, so 46 of 64 (71.9%) have missed at least once. Only 18 (28.1%) never have.

Illegal dumping is widespread: 49 of 64 (76.6%) have seen it in the last three months (Figure 26). Residents are willing to act if it is easy: 41 (64.1%) would report dumping if it took under a minute and 17 (26.6%) might, while only 6 (9.4%) would not (Figure 27). This set the design target of a one-screen report.

Reminders were the most wanted feature (Figure 28), chosen by 27 (42.2%), followed by reporting (15, 23.4%), the drop-off map (12, 18.8%) and bulky-waste dates (10, 15.6%). Reminders were therefore placed on Home and given their own screen. Demand for the app is strong: 52 (81.2%) would install it and only 3 (4.7%) would not (Table 18).

The interview supports and explains the survey. The volunteer said residents who “rent or just moved here” ask most about collection days, matching the 40.6% who rely on neighbours. She described photos shared “with no location”, which is why BinWise Belize adds a GPS pin and a category, and asked for a way to “mark it done”, which became the Sent, Seen, Cleared status. Her warning that people will not bother if reporting takes “more than a minute” agrees with the 64.1% who would report only under that condition. Her request for Spanish and Kriol matches Q10, where 23 respondents chose Spanish.

In conclusion, both instruments show a real information gap and a willingness to use a simple app, which justified building BinWise Belize. The sample is small (64) and self-selected, so the figures indicate a trend rather than measure the whole island.
