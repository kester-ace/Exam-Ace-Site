Who reports to whom?

An organisational chart is a diagram showing the hierarchy and reporting relationships within an organisation, from senior leadership down to individual staff positions.

Your SBA portfolio needs either a flow chart or an organisational chart, displayed on A4 landscape paper with narrow margins and a correct layout. Learn the rules, then build charts for five Caribbean organisations.

Mode 1 · Learn

Understanding organisational charts

Example: Sunrise Bakery, Paramaribo. Solid lines are line relationships; the dotted line is a staff relationship.

Structure & levels
Positions are arranged in levels (tiers). The most senior post is at the top; each level down has less authority.
Line of authority / chain of command
The path along which instructions travel down and reports travel up, e.g. Pastry Chef → Production Manager → Owner.
Span of control
The number of subordinates who report directly to one manager. Wide span = many direct reports; narrow span = few.
Departments
Staff are grouped by function (e.g. Production, Sales), each led by a manager.
Line vs staff relationships
Line: direct authority between a superior and a subordinate (solid line). Staff: a specialist who advises or supports but has no authority over line workers (dotted line), e.g. an Accountant or Legal Adviser.
Lateral relationship
Positions on the same level, e.g. two department managers. Neither has authority over the other.

Uses of an organisational chart

  • Shows who reports to whom
  • Makes job titles and responsibilities clear
  • Helps new staff during induction
  • Shows channels of communication
  • Helps managers plan, delegate and restructure
  • Reveals gaps or duplicated roles
  • Shows customers and suppliers whom to contact

Hierarchical (vertical / tall)

Many levels and narrow spans of control. Authority flows clearly from the top down. Common in large organisations, government bodies and schools. + close supervision; − slow communication through many levels.

Horizontal (flat)

Few levels and wide spans of control, typical of small businesses. + fast decisions and communication; − a manager may be stretched by many direct reports.

Matrix

Staff report to two managers: a functional (department) manager and a project manager. Used by firms that run projects across departments. + shares expertise; − two bosses can cause conflict.

Formatting rules for your EDPM chart

  • 1Landscape orientation. Charts are wide, so turn the page: Layout › Orientation › Landscape.
  • 2Narrow margins. Gives the chart room: Layout › Margins › Narrow (1.27 cm / 0.5″ on all sides).
  • 3A title. Centred and bold above the chart, naming the organisation, e.g. Organisational Chart of Crown Dam Mining Inc.
  • 4Consistent shapes. Same shape, size, font and style for every box (colour may mark a level, but use it consistently).
  • 5Connectors that don't cross. Use elbow connectors and keep each manager's subordinates grouped directly beneath them.
  • 6Correct layout. Boxes on the same level are aligned in a row, superiors sit above subordinates, siblings appear left to right in the order given, and the chart is centred on the page.

Method A · SmartArt in Word

  1. Insert › SmartArt › Hierarchy, choose Organization Chart (or a Hierarchy layout).
  2. Type job titles in the Text Pane; Tab demotes a box a level, Shift+Tab promotes it.
  3. Use SmartArt Design › Add Shape (After, Before, Below, Above, Assistant). Assistant gives a staff position.
  4. Delete unused boxes, apply one SmartArt style to all, and add your title above.

Method B · Shapes and connectors

  1. Insert › Shapes › Rectangle; draw one box, format it, then copy it so every box matches.
  2. Join boxes with Lines › Elbow Connector, snapping to the connection points.
  3. Line boxes up with Shape Format › Align › Align Middle / Distribute Horizontally.
  4. Select all and Group so the chart moves as one; centre it on the page.
